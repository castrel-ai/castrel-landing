Storage never stops growing, but nobody can tell you exactly when it runs out.

That is the core problem behind disk capacity management. Business data accumulates every day, yet most teams have no reliable way to quantify how much runway is left, or to tell a normal growth curve apart from an abnormal spike. The usual result is a reactive posture: watch the dashboard, wait for the threshold alert, then scramble. Capacity planning should instead start from a quantified growth baseline for storage — one that lets teams move from firefighting to proactive planning, data governance, and risk mitigation before capacity actually runs out.

## The real problem: capacity management is a lagging indicator

Most teams manage disk capacity through periodic manual inspection combined with static threshold alerts. An engineer logs into a monitoring platform or a server, runs a disk-usage query, and checks utilization across partitions. When usage crosses a preset threshold, an alert fires. Someone then logs into the host, digs through directories to find large files, and judges — mostly by experience — whether the growth is ordinary business accumulation or something else, like log or backup buildup.

If the conclusion is that more capacity is needed, the next step is a rough manual estimate of how much, followed by a budget and procurement request. If the issue looks temporary, the fix is manual cleanup: expired logs, stale backups, orphaned files. When the response comes too late, a partition fills up, and the business feels it directly — write failures, a database that hangs, an incident that needs firefighting.

This model has three structural weaknesses:

- It only observes the current state. It cannot quantify when a partition will actually run out.
- It cannot separate steady, expected growth from a sudden abnormal spike, so alerts pile up without a way to prioritize them.
- Capacity requests have no time-series evidence behind them, so budget and resourcing conversations take longer than they should.

As the number of instances grows, the manual inspection workload grows with it, and blind spots appear. Teams end up cycling through "alert → temporary cleanup → alert again" while the risk of a business-impacting outage stays in the background the whole time.

## How Castrel turns raw metrics into a capacity baseline

In a typical scenario, an operations team wants to know whether current disk growth across their data centers is sustainable, and which ones need attention first. Instead of manually pulling numbers from each host, Castrel reads the relevant Prometheus metric directly — for example `disk_capacity_bytes{datacenter="phx1"}` — for every data center in scope, over a multi-week training window.

![Castrel reads two months of Prometheus disk-capacity data and runs a Holt-Winters forecast, producing a 30-day projection with a 95% confidence band, actual values, and a forecast overlay for the training period.](https://on72nnontavb3pup.public.blob.vercel-storage.com/images/blog/4.disk-capacity-forecast/4-screenshot-en.png)

For this run, Castrel used roughly two months of daily samples (2025-07-01 to 2025-08-30, sampled every 86,400 seconds) as the training window, applied a Holt-Winters model with a 7-day seasonal period, and produced a 30-day forecast (2025-09-01 to 2025-09-30) at a 95% confidence interval. The output is not a single number. It is a forecast curve, an upper and lower bound, and — critically — a comparison against what actually happened once that period arrived, so the model's accuracy is visible rather than assumed.

## The report: which sites tracked natural growth, and which had a capacity change

The value of this workflow shows up once the forecast is checked against the actual data centers, one at a time.

![The generated capacity report breaks total capacity and each data center down by predicted vs. actual month-end value, flags which sites diverged from forecast, and preserves per-metric daily tables so the reasoning can be reviewed later.](https://on72nnontavb3pup.public.blob.vercel-storage.com/images/blog/4.disk-capacity-forecast/3-demo-en.gif)

Across the six data centers in scope, the report reached a clear, differentiated conclusion instead of a single pass/fail verdict:

| Data center | Forecast (month-end) | Actual (month-end) | Read |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| phx1 | 155.82万 TB | 156.07万 TB | Forecast and actual stayed close all month; one of the best-fitting sites |
| sac0 | 102.12万 TB | 100.91万 TB | Actual slightly below forecast, overall consistent |
| yyz1 | 7.68万 TB | 7.68万 TB | Nearly identical to forecast, the most stable site |
| ams5 | 40.64万 TB | 46.79万 TB | Actual stepped above the forecast band from mid-September — the site had capacity added manually during that window |
| iad1 | 88.37万 TB | 93.36万 TB | Actual moved past the upper confidence bound after September 17 — the same site also had manual disk additions in that period |
| sac2 | 98.29万 TB | 99.99万 TB | A late-August drop in the historical data (from a manual disk decommission) led the model to forecast a decline, but capacity held steady afterward |

At the aggregate level, total capacity was forecast to end the month around 493.62万 TB; the actual figure came in around 504.80万 TB. But that single aggregate number would have hidden the real story. Three sites — `phx1`, `sac0`, and `yyz1` — had no manual capacity changes during the period, and their actuals tracked the forecast almost exactly, confirming the model's accuracy on organic growth. The other three sites — `ams5`, `iad1`, and `sac2` — show up as flagged in the report because operators added or removed disk capacity manually during that same window. That kind of manual change shifts the underlying numbers directly; it is not a case of the model misjudging the trend. In other words, absent a manual capacity change, the model's read on organic growth holds.

That is a distinction a static threshold alert cannot make. A threshold only tells you a number crossed a line. This report tells you which sites are growing exactly as expected under organic conditions, and which ones show a divergence that traces back to a specific manual capacity change — two different situations that call for two different responses: no action needed for the former, and a quick check against the change log for the latter, not a second-guessing of the model itself.

## What this replaces in daily operations

Before this workflow, answering "will phx1 run out of space, and when" meant someone manually charting historical usage, eyeballing a trend line, and making a judgment call with no confidence interval attached. Answering "was the ams5 growth caused by a manual change" meant waiting for a threshold alert and then digging through change logs after the fact.

With the forecast in place, both questions have a standing answer: a quantified trajectory per data center, a confidence band that defines what counts as normal variation, and an explicit flag the moment actual usage steps outside that band — which is also the starting point for tracing a manual capacity change. The report also preserves the full daily table behind each site's conclusion — predicted value, confidence bounds, and actual value for every sampled day — so a reviewer can check the reasoning instead of taking the summary on faith.

## Why this matters

Disk capacity forecasting is not just a chart. It replaces a subjective, after-the-fact judgment call with a quantified, reviewable growth baseline for every data center in scope. That baseline is what makes proactive capacity planning possible: budget conversations can point to an actual trend line instead of an estimate, and the moment actual usage steps outside the confidence band, the team can immediately trace it to the specific manual expansion or decommission that caused it — without second-guessing the model itself.

The same pattern extends beyond disk capacity. Any resource with a metric history and a predictable growth pattern — memory, connection pools, queue depth, log volume — can be run through the same forecast-and-compare workflow, turning "we'll deal with it when the alert fires" into "we already know where this is heading, and if it diverges, we can trace it straight to the manual change that caused it."
